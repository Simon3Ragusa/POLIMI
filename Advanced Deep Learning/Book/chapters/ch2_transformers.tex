\chapter{Sequential Modeling and Transformers}
\label{ch:transformers}

The evolution of sequence modeling marks one of the most profound paradigm shifts in modern Deep Learning. Historically, sequence-to-sequence (seq2seq) tasks—such as Machine Translation, Image Captioning, and Time-Series Forecasting—were dominated by Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) and their gated variants, Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks and Gated Recurrent Units (GRUs). However, the sequential nature of these architectures imposed strict limits on parallelization and struggled with long-range dependencies. This chapter traces the evolution from basic Conditional Language Models to the Attention mechanism, culminating in the Transformer architecture and its subsequent adaptation to the visual domain via Vision Transformers (ViT) and Contrastive Language-Image Pre-Training (CLIP).

%-------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Sequence to Sequence Learning}
\label{sec:seq2seq}

Sequential data problems can be taxonomized based on the cardinality of their inputs and outputs:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{One-to-Many}: A single input produces a sequence (e.g., Image Captioning, where an image yields a sentence).
    \item \textbf{Many-to-One}: A sequence produces a single output (e.g., Sentiment Analysis, classifying a tweet as positive or negative).
    \item \textbf{Many-to-Many}: A sequence maps to another sequence. This can be \textit{synced} (e.g., Video Classification frame-by-frame) or \textit{asynchronous} (e.g., Machine Translation, where the output length differs from the input length).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Conditional Language Models}
A standard Language Model represents the joint probability of a sequence of tokens $y_1, \dots, y_n$ as a product of conditional probabilities:
\begin{equation}
    P(y_1, y_2, \dots, y_n) = \prod_{t=1}^n P(y_t | y_{<t})
\end{equation}

In seq2seq tasks, we condition this generation on a \textit{source sequence} (or image) $x = x_1, x_2, \dots, x_m$. Thus, we define a \textbf{Conditional Language Model}:
\begin{equation}
    P(y_1, \dots, y_n | x_1, \dots, x_m) = \prod_{t=1}^n P(y_t | y_{<t}, x_1, \dots, x_m)
\end{equation}

During training, we provide the model with pairs of source sequences $S$ and target sequences $T$. The model is trained via \textbf{Teacher Forcing}, where the true previous tokens are fed into the network to predict the next token $y_t$. The objective is to minimize the Cross-Entropy loss over the batch:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_t(p_t, y_t) = -\sum_{i=1}^{|V|} y_t^{(i)} \log(p_t^{(i)}) = -y_t^T \log(p_t)
\end{equation}
where $p_t = p(\cdot | y_{<t}, x)$ is the predicted probability distribution over the vocabulary $V$. Over the entire sequence, the loss becomes $-\sum_{t=0}^n y_t^T \log(p_t)$.

\subsection{Greedy Decoding vs. Beam Search}
At inference time, the true target sequence is unknown. To find the optimal sequence $y^* = \arg\max_y P(y|x)$, we face a computationally intractable search space of $|V|^n$.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Greedy Decoding}: At each step $t$, the model simply picks the token with the highest probability. However, this local optimum does not guarantee a global optimum; a sub-optimal choice at step $t=1$ cannot be undone and cascades into future steps.
    \item \textbf{Beam Search}: Instead of keeping only the best token, the model maintains a ``beam'' of the top $K$ most probable partial sequences (hypotheses) at each step. This allows the model to backtrack effectively if a seemingly good early choice leads to a dead end.
\end{itemize}

\section{The Encoder-Decoder Bottleneck and Attention}
\label{sec:attention}

The classical approach to seq2seq learning utilized an \textbf{Encoder-Decoder} architecture built upon LSTMs. The Encoder processes the source sequence $x$ step-by-step, compressing it into a \textit{single fixed-size context vector} (the final hidden state). The Decoder then uses this single vector to autoregressively generate the target sequence.

This creates a severe \textbf{representation bottleneck}. The Encoder is forced to compress all semantic meaning of a potentially long sentence into one vector. Furthermore, different parts of the source sentence are relevant at different time steps during decoding.

\subsection{The Attention Mechanism}
To bypass this bottleneck, the \textbf{Attention mechanism} allows the Decoder to "look back" at the entire sequence of Encoder hidden states and dynamically decide which states are most relevant for the current decoding step.

At decoder step $t$ with hidden state $h_t$, and encoder states $s_1, \dots, s_m$, we compute an \textbf{attention score} for each encoder token:
\begin{equation}
    \text{score}(h_t, s_k) \quad \text{for } k = 1 \dots m
\end{equation}
These scores are normalized using a Softmax function to produce \textbf{attention weights} $\alpha_k^{(t)}$:
\begin{equation}
    \alpha_k^{(t)} = \frac{\exp(\text{score}(h_t, s_k))}{\sum_{i=1}^m \exp(\text{score}(h_t, s_i))}
\end{equation}
Finally, the \textbf{context vector} $c^{(t)}$ for step $t$ is computed as the weighted sum of the encoder states:
\begin{equation}
    c^{(t)} = \sum_{k=1}^m \alpha_k^{(t)} s_k
\end{equation}

\subsection{Computing Attention Scores}
Several mechanisms exist to compute the alignment score between the decoder state $h_t$ and the encoder state $s_k$:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dot-Product}: $\text{score}(h_t, s_k) = h_t^T s_k$. Fast and parameter-free, but requires $h_t$ and $s_k$ to have the same dimensionality.
    \item \textbf{Bilinear (Luong Attention)}: $\text{score}(h_t, s_k) = h_t^T W s_k$. Introduces a learnable weight matrix $W$ to project the spaces.
    \item \textbf{Multi-Layer Perceptron (Bahdanau Attention)}: $\text{score}(h_t, s_k) = w_2^T \tanh(W_1 [h_t ; s_k])$. Concatenates the states and passes them through a neural network.
\end{itemize}

\begin{insightbox}[Attention as Soft Alignment]
Traditional machine translation relied on hard dictionaries (e.g., word $X$ translates to word $Y$). Attention acts as a differentiable, ``soft'' dictionary. When generating the French word ``pomme'', the attention mechanism assigns a weight of 0.95 to the English encoder state for ``apple'' and near 0.0 to the rest of the sentence. This essentially learns word alignment automatically without any explicit alignment labels.
\end{insightbox}

\section{The Transformer: Attention Is All You Need}
\label{sec:transformer_arch}

While LSTMs with Attention achieved State-of-The-Art (SOTA) results, the NLP community hit a performance ceiling. The fundamental flaw of RNNs is their \textbf{sequential nature}: to compute state $h_t$, you must first compute $h_{t-1}$. This precludes parallelization across tokens, making training on massive datasets prohibitively slow. 

In 2017, Google introduced the \textbf{Transformer}, a purely attention-based architecture that completely discards recurrence and convolutions, allowing for massive parallelization.

\subsection{Self-Attention and QKV formulation}
Instead of computing attention between a decoder and an encoder, \textbf{Self-Attention} computes attention between tokens within the same sequence. Every token looks at every other token to update its own representation based on the surrounding context.

This is formalized using the \textbf{Query, Key, Value (QKV)} paradigm, borrowed from database retrieval:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Query ($Q$)}: What information am I looking for? (Computed as $X W_Q$)
    \item \textbf{Key ($K$)}: What information do I contain? (Computed as $X W_K$)
    \item \textbf{Value ($V$)}: Here is my actual content. (Computed as $X W_V$)
\end{itemize}

The output is computed using Scaled Dot-Product Attention:
\begin{equation}
    \text{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \text{softmax}\left( \frac{Q K^T}{\sqrt{d_k}} \right) V
\end{equation}
The dot product $Q K^T$ computes the raw similarity scores between all queries and all keys. We scale by $\frac{1}{\sqrt{d_k}}$ to prevent the dot products from growing too large and pushing the softmax function into regions with extremely small gradients. The result is then multiplied by $V$ to aggregate the relevant information.

\begin{insightbox}[Queries, Keys, and Values]
Imagine you are at a library looking for a book on Deep Learning. 
Your \textbf{Query} is the search term you type into the computer: "Neural Networks".
The \textbf{Keys} are the titles and metadata printed on the spine of every book on the shelf. 
The dot product $Q K^T$ calculates how well your search term matches the title of each book. 
Once you find the book with the highest match (highest softmax probability), you open it and read the text inside—that text is the \textbf{Value}. In Self-Attention, every word in the sentence simultaneously acts as a Query, a Key, and a Value.
\end{insightbox}

\subsection{Multi-Head Attention}
A single attention mechanism might focus heavily on just one aspect (e.g., adjacent words). To allow the model to jointly attend to information from different representation subspaces, the Transformer uses \textbf{Multi-Head Attention}. The queries, keys, and values are linearly projected $h$ times into smaller dimensions, attention is performed in parallel, and the results are concatenated and projected back:
\begin{equation}
    \text{MultiHead}(Q,K,V) = \text{Concat}(\text{head}_1, \dots, \text{head}_h) W_O
\end{equation}
where $\text{head}_i = \text{Attention}(Q W_Q^{(i)}, K W_K^{(i)}, V W_V^{(i)})$.

Empirical analysis reveals that these heads learn interpretable, specialized roles:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Positional Heads}: Attend to immediate neighbors (e.g., $i-1$ or $i+1$).
    \item \textbf{Syntactic Heads}: Track major grammatical relations (subject-verb, verb-object).
    \item \textbf{Rare Token Heads}: Focus specifically on the least frequent tokens in the sentence.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Positional Encoding}
Because the Transformer processes all tokens in parallel using matrix multiplication, it possesses no inherent notion of sequence order. The operation is \textbf{permutation invariant}.

To inject order, we add a \textbf{Positional Encoding} to the input embeddings before they enter the network. The original Transformer uses fixed sinusoidal functions:
\begin{align}
    PE_{(pos, 2i)} &= \sin(pos / 10000^{2i/d_{model}}) \\
    PE_{(pos, 2i+1)} &= \cos(pos / 10000^{2i/d_{model}})
\end{align}
where $pos$ is the position in the sequence, and $i$ is the dimension index. 

By the trigonometric addition theorems, for any fixed offset $k$, $PE_{pos+k}$ can be represented as a linear function of $PE_{pos}$. This allows the model to easily learn relative positioning. While some modern architectures (like BERT) opt for fully learnable positional embeddings, the sinusoidal approach remains highly effective and extrapolates well to sequence lengths unseen during training.

\subsection{Transformer Architecture and Complexity}
A full Transformer block consists of:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Multi-Head Attention} (Masked in the decoder to prevent looking into the future during autoregressive generation).
    \item \textbf{Layer Normalization} and \textbf{Residual Connections} around the attention block to stabilize deep gradients.
    \item A position-wise \textbf{Feed-Forward Network (FFN)} with ReLU activation: $\text{FFN}(x) = \max(0, xW_1 + b_1)W_2 + b_2$.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Complexity Analysis:}
RNNs require $O(n)$ sequential operations, causing a bottleneck. Transformers require $O(1)$ sequential operations, enabling maximum GPU utilization. However, Self-Attention computes a dense $n \times n$ matrix for $Q K^T$, yielding a complexity of $O(n^2 \cdot d)$. Thus, while Transformers are infinitely parallelizable, their memory and computational requirements scale quadratically with the sequence length $n$, prompting extensive research into sparse and efficient attention mechanisms.

\section{Vision Transformers (ViT)}
\label{sec:vit}

Given the utter dominance of Transformers in NLP (spawning BERT for bidirectional encoding and GPT for autoregressive generation), the computer vision community naturally sought to apply them to images. 

Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) rely heavily on strict \textbf{inductive biases}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Locality (Spatial Bias)}: Convolutions assume that neighboring pixels are highly correlated.
    \item \textbf{Translation Equivariance}: A feature learned in the top-left corner is computed identically in the bottom-right corner.
\end{itemize}

However, CNNs suffer when dealing with \textbf{long-distance dependencies}. Due to their localized kernel sizes, a CNN's \textit{receptive field} only grows slowly as network depth increases. Furthermore, the \textit{effective receptive field} (ERF) heavily decays at the edges, meaning a CNN struggles to establish a direct relationship between pixels on opposite sides of an image.

\subsection{The ViT Architecture}
The \textbf{Vision Transformer (ViT)} (Dosovitskiy et al., 2020) proved that an image is quite literally ``worth $16 \times 16$ words.'' ViT proudly abandons image-specific inductive biases, utilizing a pure Transformer architecture.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Patch Extraction}: An image $I \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times W \times C}$ is divided into a grid of non-overlapping $P \times P$ patches. The number of patches is $N = HW / P^2$.
    \item \textbf{Linear Projection}: Each patch is flattened into a vector and passed through a learnable linear layer to yield a $D$-dimensional embedding.
    \item \textbf{Positional Embedding}: Because transformers are permutation invariant, a 1D learnable positional embedding is added to each patch embedding to retain spatial awareness.
    \item \textbf{Class Token}: A special learnable `[CLASS]` token is prepended to the sequence. After passing through the Transformer layers, the state of this specific token is passed to an MLP head to output the final classification prediction.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{insightbox}[The Cost of Dropping Inductive Biases]
Because ViT does not assume locality or translation equivariance, it has to learn all spatial relationships from scratch. If you train a ViT on a "mid-size" dataset like ImageNet-1K (1 million images), it will underperform compared to a ResNet. However, if you train it on a massive dataset like JFT-300M (300 million images), the Transformer's lack of restrictive priors allows its massive capacity to shine, outperforming highly optimized CNNs.
\end{insightbox}

\subsection{Drawbacks and Hierarchical Transformers}
Despite their success, standard ViTs have significant drawbacks:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Quadratic Complexity}: High-resolution images produce thousands of patches, making the $O(n^2)$ global self-attention intractable.
    \item \textbf{Fixed Scales}: Tokens have a fixed size, making dense prediction tasks (like object detection and semantic segmentation) exceedingly difficult.
\end{itemize}

To solve this, architectures like the \textbf{Swin Transformer} (Liu et al., 2021) introduce hierarchical feature maps. Swin computes self-attention only within local shifted windows (linear complexity) and merges patches in deeper layers to create multi-scale representations, bridging the gap between CNN efficiency and Transformer capacity.

\section{Multi-Modal Learning: CLIP and LSeg}
\label{sec:clip_lseg}

Moving beyond closed-set classification (where the model is restricted to a fixed set of predefined classes), we encounter \textbf{Open-Set} and \textbf{Zero-Shot} paradigms.

\subsection{Contrastive Language-Image Pre-Training (CLIP)}
\textbf{CLIP} (Radford et al., ICML 2021) revolutionized zero-shot classification by leveraging natural language supervision. Instead of predicting a categorical label, CLIP is trained on 400 million (image, text) pairs scraped from the internet.

It jointly trains two encoders:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item An Image Encoder (e.g., ViT or ResNet).
    \item A Text Encoder (e.g., BERT-like Transformer).
\end{enumerate}

Given a batch of $N$ (image, text) pairs, CLIP computes the embeddings for all images and all texts. The training objective is to maximize the cosine similarity of the $N$ correct pairings (the diagonal of the $N \times N$ similarity matrix) while simultaneously minimizing the similarity of the $N^2 - N$ incorrect pairings using a symmetric \textbf{Contrastive Loss}.

\textbf{Zero-Shot Classification}: At inference time, to classify an image, we pass the image through the Image Encoder. We then generate text strings for all possible classes (e.g., "A photo of a dog", "A photo of a cat") and pass them through the Text Encoder. The class whose text embedding has the highest cosine similarity with the image embedding is selected. This allows CLIP to classify objects it was never explicitly trained to recognize, effectively turning classification into a cross-modal retrieval task.

\subsection{LSeg: Language-Driven Semantic Segmentation}
Building upon CLIP, \textbf{LSeg} (Li et al., ICLR 2022) adapts this multi-modal approach for dense predictions. While standard segmentation models output a predefined channel dimension for each pixel, LSeg uses a ViT image encoder trained to yield dense pixel-level activations that mathematically align with the CLIP embeddings of text queries.

This allows users to perform \textit{zero-shot semantic segmentation}. At test time, a user can input novel classes (e.g., "furry", "plant", "stone") that were never seen during training, and the network will segment the image by computing the dot product between the dense image features and the CLIP text embeddings of those arbitrary words.

\section{Chapter Overview}

\subsection{Summary}
This chapter chronicles the evolution of sequence modeling from foundational RNN-based Encoder-Decoder architectures to the highly parallelizable Transformer. We discussed how early sequence-to-sequence learning suffered from a representation bottleneck, leading to the introduction of the Attention mechanism to enable dynamic, soft alignment between source and target tokens. Building upon this, the Transformer architecture entirely discarded recurrence in favor of Self-Attention and Multi-Head Attention, overcoming previous limitations in parallelization. Finally, we explored the extension of these architectures to the visual domain and multi-modal learning, highlighting Vision Transformers (ViT), Swin Transformers, CLIP, and LSeg, which unlocked new capabilities in zero-shot classification and semantic segmentation.

\subsection{Possible Exam Questions}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Q: Explain the fundamental difference between Teacher Forcing and inference (Greedy Decoding vs. Beam Search) in seq2seq models.} \\
    \textbf{A:} Teacher Forcing uses the true previous tokens during training to predict the next token, whereas during inference, the model must rely on its own generated tokens. Greedy Decoding selects the single most probable token at each step (potentially leading to sub-optimal sequences), while Beam Search maintains the top $K$ partial sequences, allowing the model to explore multiple hypotheses and backtrack if needed.
    
    \item \textbf{Q: What is the ``Encoder-Decoder Bottleneck'', and how does the Attention mechanism solve it?} \\
    \textbf{A:} The bottleneck occurs because the standard Encoder-Decoder architecture compresses the entire source sequence into a single fixed-size context vector, losing information for long sequences. Attention solves this by allowing the Decoder to dynamically ``look back'' at all Encoder hidden states and compute a weighted sum (context vector) for each decoding step based on learned attention scores.

    \item \textbf{Q: Describe the QKV (Query, Key, Value) paradigm in the context of Self-Attention. What is the role of the $\sqrt{d_k}$ scaling factor?} \\
    \textbf{A:} In Self-Attention, each token acts as a Query (what to look for), a Key (what it contains), and a Value (its actual content). Attention scores are computed via the dot product of Queries and Keys, then multiplied by Values. The $\sqrt{d_k}$ scaling factor prevents the dot products from growing too large, which would push the softmax function into regions with extremely small gradients.

    \item \textbf{Q: How does Multi-Head Attention differ from single-head attention, and what specialized roles can different heads learn?} \\
    \textbf{A:} Multi-Head Attention projects the queries, keys, and values into multiple smaller subspaces and computes attention in parallel, allowing the model to jointly attend to different types of relationships. Different heads often specialize into positional heads (attending to neighbors), syntactic heads (tracking grammatical relations), and rare token heads.

    \item \textbf{Q: Why do Transformers require Positional Encoding, and how is it typically implemented?} \\
    \textbf{A:} Since Transformers process all tokens in parallel and lack recurrence or convolutions, their operations are permutation invariant and have no inherent sense of sequence order. Positional Encoding injects order by adding structured vectors (typically using fixed sinusoidal functions of varying frequencies) to the input embeddings.

    \item \textbf{Q: Compare the inductive biases of Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) with those of Vision Transformers (ViTs).} \\
    \textbf{A:} CNNs heavily rely on spatial locality (neighboring pixels are correlated) and translation equivariance (features are computed identically regardless of position). ViTs abandon these restrictive image-specific biases in favor of a pure attention mechanism, treating images as sequences of patches. This requires massive data to learn spatial relationships but offers higher capacity without local constraints.

    \item \textbf{Q: Discuss the primary drawbacks of the standard ViT architecture and how hierarchical models like the Swin Transformer address them.} \\
    \textbf{A:} Standard ViTs have quadratic complexity with respect to the number of patches, making them intractable for high-resolution images, and they produce fixed-scale tokens unsuitable for dense prediction tasks. The Swin Transformer addresses this by using local shifted windows (restoring linear complexity) and merging patches in deeper layers to create hierarchical, multi-scale feature maps.

    \item \textbf{Q: How does the Contrastive Loss function work in CLIP, and how does it enable zero-shot classification?} \\
    \textbf{A:} CLIP trains Image and Text Encoders to maximize the cosine similarity of correct image-text pairs while minimizing it for incorrect pairs in a batch using a symmetric Contrastive Loss. At inference, zero-shot classification is achieved by comparing the image embedding with text embeddings of all candidate class names (e.g., "A photo of a [class]") and picking the one with the highest similarity.
\end{itemize}
