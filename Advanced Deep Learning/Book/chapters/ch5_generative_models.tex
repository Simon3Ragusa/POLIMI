\chapter{Deep Generative Models: GANs and VAEs} \label{ch:gans_vaes}

\section{Introduction to Generative Models}
\index{Generative Models}
Generative models aim to capture the underlying data distribution of a given training set $TR = \{x_i\}$, such that they can generate new, previously unseen samples that are similar to those in $TR$. 
Images reside in a highly complex and difficult-to-describe manifold within a huge-dimensional space. Generative models represent the "holy grail" of image processing as they learn to model this distribution directly or implicitly.

Applications of generative models encompass data augmentation, simulation, and planning. Furthermore, they are pivotal in solving inverse problems such as super-resolution, inpainting, and colorization. Training these models also yields rich latent representations that serve as powerful general features.

\begin{insightbox}[The Intuition behind Generative Modeling]
Rather than simply mapping an input to a label (as in discriminative modeling), generative modeling attempts to understand the entire structure of the data. Imagine a high-dimensional space where every possible image is a point. Meaningful images (like faces or handwritten digits) lie on a much lower-dimensional, convoluted surface---a manifold---within this space. A generative model maps a simple, known distribution (like a standard Gaussian) to this complex manifold, allowing us to randomly sample from the Gaussian and consistently land on a valid, realistic image.
\end{insightbox}

\section{From Autoencoders to Variational Autoencoders (VAEs)}
\index{Variational Autoencoders (VAEs)}
\index{Autoencoders}

\subsection{Recap of Autoencoders}
Standard autoencoders are neural networks designed for unsupervised data reconstruction. They consist of an encoder $\mathcal{E}$ and a decoder $\mathcal{D}$. For an input $s \in \mathbb{R}^n$, the encoder produces a latent representation $z = \mathcal{E}(s)$ with $z \in \mathbb{R}^d$, where $d \ll n$. The decoder then attempts to reconstruct the input as $\hat{s} = \mathcal{D}(z)$. The model is trained by minimizing a reconstruction loss, typically the Mean Squared Error (MSE):
\begin{equation} \label{eq:ae_reconstruction}
    \ell(S) = \sum_{s \in S} || s - \mathcal{D}(\mathcal{E}(s)) ||_2
\end{equation}
While standard autoencoders learn an identity mapping through a compact bottleneck, they cannot be readily used as generative models. To generate novel samples, one would need to draw a random vector $z \sim \phi_z$ and feed it to the decoder $\mathcal{D}$. However, the true distribution of the latent space $\phi_z$ is unknown and highly irregular. Sampling from an arbitrary region often yields meaningless noise, as the decoder has only been trained to map specific encoded points back to data.

\subsection{Variational Autoencoders}
To formulate a generative process, Variational Autoencoders (VAEs) add a probabilistic spin. We enforce the latent features $z$ to follow a known prior distribution, typically a standard Gaussian $\phi_z = \mathcal{N}(0, \mathbb{I})$. 

Our goal is to maximize the likelihood of the generated images, $p_\theta(x)$, parameterized by $\theta$. By integrating over all possible latent variables, we obtain:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:vae_likelihood}
    p_\theta(x) = \int p_\theta(x, z) dz = \int p_\theta(x|z) p(z) dz
\end{equation}
where $p_\theta(x|z)$ is the decoder output distribution and $p(z)$ is the Gaussian prior. Because evaluating this integral over all $z$ is intractable, VAEs employ variational inference. We introduce an approximation $q_\phi(z|x)$ parameterized by a neural network---the encoder $\mathcal{E}_\phi$---to approximate the intractable posterior.

Instead of predicting a deterministic vector $z$, the encoder $\mathcal{E}_\phi$ predicts the parameters of a distribution. Specifically, it outputs the expectation $\mu \in \mathbb{R}^d$ and the diagonal covariance matrix $\Sigma \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$. 

\begin{insightbox}[The Reparameterization Trick]
To train the encoder and decoder jointly via backpropagation algorithms like SGD, the sampling operation must be differentiable. Since we cannot analytically backpropagate through a random node $z \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \Sigma)$, we reparameterize $z$. We draw a noise sample from a standard Gaussian $\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \mathbb{I})$ and compute $z$ as a deterministic transformation: $z = \mu + \epsilon \odot \Sigma$. This trick decouples the randomness from the network parameters, allowing gradients to flow back into $\mu$ and $\Sigma$.
\end{insightbox}

\subsection{The VAE Loss (ELBO)}
\index{ELBO}
\index{Kullback-Leibler divergence}
By applying Jensen's inequality, we maximize a tractable lower bound on the log-likelihood, known as the Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO):
\begin{equation} \label{eq:vae_elbo}
    \log(p_{\theta,\phi}(x)) \geq \mathbb{E}_{z \sim q_\phi(z|x)}[\log(p_\theta(x|z))] - \text{KL}(q_\phi(z|x), p(z))
\end{equation}
This yields the VAE training loss, which consists of two terms:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Reconstruction Term}: $\mathbb{E}_{z \sim q_\phi(z|x)}[\log(p_\theta(x|z))]$, which practically translates to minimizing the Mean Squared Error (MSE) between the input $x$ and the reconstruction $\mathcal{D}_\theta(z)$.
    \item \textbf{Regularization Term}: $\text{KL}(q_\phi(z|x), p(z))$, the Kullback-Leibler divergence between the encoder's predicted distribution and the standard Gaussian prior $\mathcal{N}(0, \mathbb{I})$. It acts as a regularizer, forcing the latent space to adhere to the prior. For Gaussians, this term possesses a closed-form analytical solution.
\end{enumerate}

During inference, generation is performed by discarding the encoder entirely, drawing a random sample $z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \mathbb{I})$, and passing it through the decoder $\mathcal{D}_\theta$.

\section{Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs)}
\index{Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs)}

Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) take a radically different approach. Instead of explicitly modeling the density function, GANs aim to produce samples that are indistinguishable from the true data distribution. 

A GAN consists of two competing neural networks:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Generator ($\mathcal{G}$)}: Takes as input a random noise vector $z \sim \phi_z$ and maps it to the data space, producing a synthetic image $\mathcal{G}(z, \theta_g)$. Its goal is to generate samples realistic enough to fool the discriminator.
    \item \textbf{Discriminator ($\mathcal{D}$)}: Takes an image $s \in \mathbb{R}^n$ (either real from $TR$, or fake from $\mathcal{G}$) and outputs a probability $\mathcal{D}(s, \theta_d) \in [0, 1]$ indicating whether the image is real. 
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The Minimax Game}
\index{Minimax Game}
The training process is formulated as a two-player adversarial minimax game. The discriminator $\mathcal{D}$ aims to maximize its accuracy in distinguishing real from fake, essentially maximizing the binary cross-entropy:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:gan_d_opt}
    \max_{\theta_d} \left( \mathbb{E}_{s \sim \phi_S}[\log \mathcal{D}(s, \theta_d)] + \mathbb{E}_{z \sim \phi_Z}[\log(1 - \mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G}(z, \theta_g), \theta_d))] \right)
\end{equation}
Conversely, the generator $\mathcal{G}$ aims to minimize the discriminator's success rate:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:gan_minimax}
    \min_{\theta_g} \max_{\theta_d} \left( \mathbb{E}_{s \sim \phi_S}[\log \mathcal{D}(s, \theta_d)] + \mathbb{E}_{z \sim \phi_Z}[\log(1 - \mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G}(z, \theta_g), \theta_d))] \right)
\end{equation}

\begin{insightbox}[The Adversarial Minimax Game]
Think of the Generator as an art forger attempting to paint fake masterpieces, and the Discriminator as an art detective trying to spot the fakes. Initially, the forger's paintings are just random noise, and the detective easily catches them. However, as the detective explains \textit{why} the paintings are fake (via the gradient), the forger learns and improves. Over time, the forger becomes so skilled that the detective is completely fooled, essentially reduced to guessing randomly (a 50\% success rate). At this point, the Generator produces highly realistic images.
\end{insightbox}

\subsection{Optimization and the ``GAN Trick''}
Training is performed by alternating stochastic gradient updates between $\mathcal{D}$ and $\mathcal{G}$. A well-known issue with the objective in Equation \ref{eq:gan_minimax} is that early in training, $\mathcal{G}$ is very poor. Consequently, $\mathcal{D}$ rejects samples with extremely high confidence ($\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G}(z)) \approx 0$). In this regime, the function $\log(1 - \mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G}(z)))$ is flat, leading to vanishing gradients and stalling the learning process of $\mathcal{G}$.

To circumvent this, instead of training $\mathcal{G}$ to minimize $\log(1 - \mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G}(z)))$, it is trained to maximize $\log(\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G}(z)))$. This alternative objective yields the same fixed point in the dynamics but provides a much stronger gradient signal during the early learning stages.

At inference time, the discriminator is discarded, and the generator is utilized as the generative model.

\subsection{Latent Space Interpolation and Arithmetic}
A properly trained GAN learns a meaningful continuous manifold. Performing linear interpolation between two noise vectors $z_1$ and $z_2$ generates smooth transitions in the output image space, indicating that the network hasn't simply memorized the dataset but has mapped the underlying semantic factors. 
Furthermore, the latent space exhibits semantic structure allowing for vector arithmetic. For instance, computing the average latent vector for ``smiling women'', subtracting the vector for ``neutral women'', and adding the vector for ``neutral men'' yields a generated image of a ``smiling man''.

\section{Conditional GANs and Anomaly Detection}
\index{Conditional GANs}

\subsection{Conditional GANs}
To steer the generative process towards specific outputs, one can condition the generation on auxiliary information $y$, such as class labels. In a Deep Convolutional Conditional GAN (cGAN), this information is concatenated as a one-hot-encoded vector to the input noise $z$ for the generator, and also to the images fed to the discriminator. Consequently, the discriminator checks not only if the image looks real, but also if it matches the provided condition $y$. This guarantees that $\mathcal{G}$ generates data consistent with the specified label.

\subsection{Bidirectional GANs (BiGANs) for Anomaly Detection}
\index{BiGAN}
\index{Anomaly Detection}
While GANs map $z \to x$, they lack a native mechanism to invert this mapping ($x \to z$). Bidirectional GANs (BiGANs) introduce an encoder $\mathcal{E}$ alongside the generator $\mathcal{G}$. The discriminator in a BiGAN takes pairs of $(s, z)$ and must classify whether the pair originated from the real data distribution accompanied by its encoded latent vector $(s, \mathcal{E}(s))$, or from the generator and its noise vector $(\mathcal{G}(z), z)$.

This bidirectional property makes BiGANs suitable for anomaly detection. By training the model exclusively on normal (non-anomalous) images, the network learns the manifold of standard patterns. At test time, given a new image $s$, an anomaly score $A(s)$ can be computed by combining the image reconstruction error and a feature-level distance:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:anomaly_score}
    A(s) = (1 - \alpha) || \mathcal{G}(\mathcal{E}(s)) - s ||_2 + \alpha || f(\mathcal{D}(s, \mathcal{E}(s))) - f(\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{E}(s)), \mathcal{E}(s))) ||_2
\end{equation}
where $f$ denotes feature extraction layers within the discriminator. High anomaly scores effectively localize defective or unprecedented regions in the image.
