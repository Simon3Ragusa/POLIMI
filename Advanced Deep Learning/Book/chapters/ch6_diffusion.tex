\chapter{Diffusion Models}
\label{ch:diffusion_models}

\section{Introduction to Generative AI and Diffusion}

Image generation has seen a revolutionary shift with the advent of Diffusion Models. As discussed in the context of Generative AI, the goal is to transform a random vector $\mathbf{z}$ drawn from a known distribution, such as a standard Gaussian, into a complex data sample such as a high-resolution image. While previous architectures like Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) mapping $\mathbf{z} \sim \phi_z$ to images through a Generative Neural Network achieved significant milestones, diffusion models formulate generation as an iterative denoising process. 

\begin{insightbox}[Generative AI Across Modalities]
When applied to text, generative neural networks like GPT-3 auto-regressively predict the next token, enabling tasks ranging from prose generation to rhyming mathematical proofs. In vision, systems like Midjourney or DALL-E combine text embeddings with image generation mechanisms to produce completely novel content, such as ``Pope Francis wearing a long white puffer coat.'' Diffusion models serve as the backbone for these state-of-the-art vision systems.
\end{insightbox}

\section{The Forward Diffusion Process}

The forward diffusion process, or simply the forward process, is a Markov chain that gradually adds Gaussian noise to a data point from the real data distribution $\mathbf{x}_0 \sim q(\mathbf{x})$ over $T$ steps. This produces a sequence of increasingly noisy samples $\mathbf{x}_1, \mathbf{x}_2, \dots, \mathbf{x}_T$. The step sizes are controlled by a variance schedule $\{\beta_t \in (0, 1)\}_{t=1}^T$.

The transition from state $\mathbf{x}_{t-1}$ to $\mathbf{x}_t$ is defined as:
\begin{equation}
    q(\mathbf{x}_t | \mathbf{x}_{t-1}) = \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_t; \sqrt{1 - \beta_t}\mathbf{x}_{t-1}, \beta_t \mathbf{I})
\end{equation}

Given a sufficiently large $T$ and a well-behaved schedule of $\beta_t$, the final latent variable $\mathbf{x}_T$ is nearly an isotropic Gaussian distribution, i.e., $\mathbf{x}_T \approx \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})$.

\subsection{The Reparameterization Trick and Closed-Form Sampling}

A crucial property of the forward process is that we can sample $\mathbf{x}_t$ at any arbitrary time step $t$ in a closed form using the reparameterization trick. Let $\alpha_t = 1 - \beta_t$ and $\bar{\alpha}_t = \prod_{i=1}^t \alpha_i$. We can recursively express $\mathbf{x}_t$ as:
\begin{align}
    \mathbf{x}_t &= \sqrt{\alpha_t} \mathbf{x}_{t-1} + \sqrt{1 - \alpha_t}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t-1} \\
                 &= \sqrt{\alpha_t \alpha_{t-1}} \mathbf{x}_{t-2} + \sqrt{1 - \alpha_t \alpha_{t-1}} \bar{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{t-2} \\
                 &= \dots = \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t} \mathbf{x}_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t} \boldsymbol{\epsilon}
\end{align}
where $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t-1}, \boldsymbol{\epsilon} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})$. 

\begin{insightbox}[Merging Gaussians]
Note that $\bar{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{t-2}$ merges two Gaussians with variance $\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \sigma_1^2 \mathbf{I})$ and $\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \sigma_2^2 \mathbf{I})$ into $\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, (\sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2)\mathbf{I})$. In this specific case, the merged standard deviation becomes $\sqrt{(1-\alpha_t) + \alpha_t(1-\alpha_{t-1})} = \sqrt{1 - \alpha_t \alpha_{t-1}}$.
\end{insightbox}

From this derivation, the marginal distribution of $\mathbf{x}_t$ given $\mathbf{x}_0$ is:
\begin{equation}
    q(\mathbf{x}_t | \mathbf{x}_0) = \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_t; \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}\mathbf{x}_0, (1 - \bar{\alpha}_t)\mathbf{I})
\end{equation}

\section{The Reverse Diffusion Process}

To generate new samples, we need to reverse the forward process. If we could compute $q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1} | \mathbf{x}_t)$, we could start from true noise $\mathbf{x}_T \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})$ and progressively denoise it to recreate a true sample $\mathbf{x}_0$. However, the exact reverse distribution $q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1} | \mathbf{x}_t)$ is intractable as it requires evaluating the entire data distribution. 

Instead, we learn a parameterized model $p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_{t-1} | \mathbf{x}_t)$ to approximate it. If $\beta_t$ is sufficiently small, the reverse transitions are also Gaussian. Thus, we define:
\begin{equation}
    p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_{t-1} | \mathbf{x}_t) = \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}; \boldsymbol{\mu}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t), \mathbf{\Sigma}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t))
\end{equation}
The full joint distribution of the reverse trajectory is:
\begin{equation}
    p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_{0:T}) = p(\mathbf{x}_T) \prod_{t=1}^T p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_{t-1} | \mathbf{x}_t)
\end{equation}
Common formulations simplify the learning process by fixing the variance to a time-dependent constant, $\mathbf{\Sigma}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t) = \sigma_t^2 \mathbf{I}$, such as $\sigma_t^2 = \beta_t$, and only learning the mean $\boldsymbol{\mu}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t)$.

\section{Training the Reverse Diffusion Process}

To approximate $q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1} | \mathbf{x}_t)$ with $p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_{t-1} | \mathbf{x}_t)$, we must train $\boldsymbol{\mu}_\theta$ to predict the true posterior mean. The forward posterior, conditioned on $\mathbf{x}_0$, is strictly Gaussian and derived via Bayes' theorem:
\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{\mu}_t(\mathbf{x}_t, \mathbf{x}_0) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}} \left( \mathbf{x}_t - \frac{1 - \alpha_t}{\sqrt{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t}} \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_t \right)
\end{equation}

Since $\mathbf{x}_t$ is available at training time as a function of $\mathbf{x}_0$ and noise $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$, we can reparameterize the neural network to predict the noise $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t)$ rather than predicting the mean directly:
\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{\mu}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}} \left( \mathbf{x}_t - \frac{1 - \alpha_t}{\sqrt{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t}} \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t) \right)
\end{equation}

The model is trained to minimize the difference from the observed sample, optimizing the variational lower bound (ELBO). The exact loss term for step $t$ is:
\begin{align}
    L_t &= \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_0, \boldsymbol{\epsilon}} \left[ \frac{1}{2\|\mathbf{\Sigma}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t)\|_2^2} \|\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_t(\mathbf{x}_t, \mathbf{x}_0) - \boldsymbol{\mu}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t)\|^2 \right] \\
        &= \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_0, \boldsymbol{\epsilon}} \left[ \frac{(1 - \alpha_t)^2}{2\alpha_t(1 - \bar{\alpha}_t)\|\mathbf{\Sigma}_\theta\|_2^2} \|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_t - \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t)\|^2 \right] \\
        &= \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_0, \boldsymbol{\epsilon}} \left[ \frac{(1 - \alpha_t)^2}{2\alpha_t(1 - \bar{\alpha}_t)\|\mathbf{\Sigma}_\theta\|_2^2} \|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_t - \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}\mathbf{x}_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_t, t)\|^2 \right]
\end{align}

\begin{insightbox}[Simplified Objective]
Empirically, Ho et al. (2020) found that the model trains much better and yields superior sample quality when using a simplified, unweighted formulation of the loss:
\begin{equation}
    L_t^{\text{simple}} = \mathbb{E}_{t \sim [1,T], \mathbf{x}_0, \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_t} \left[ \|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_t - \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t)\|^2 \right]
\end{equation}
This effectively downweights the loss at very small $t$, focusing the network's capacity on the more difficult denoising tasks at larger $t$.
\end{insightbox}

\section{Parametrization of Noise Variance and Schedulers}

The forward variances $\beta_t$ must be carefully designed. In the original DDPM (Ho et al., 2020), $\beta_t$ were set as linearly increasing constants from $\beta_1 = 10^{-4}$ to $\beta_T = 0.02$. While yielding high-quality images, the linear scheduler destroys information too quickly; by $t \approx T/2$, the image is already pure noise, rendering the latter half of the forward process redundant.

Nichol \& Dhariwal (2021) proposed a \textbf{cosine-based schedule}:
\begin{equation}
    \bar{\alpha}_t = \frac{f(t)}{f(0)}, \quad \text{where } f(t) = \cos\left( \frac{t/T + s}{1 + s} \cdot \frac{\pi}{2} \right)^2
\end{equation}
and $\beta_t = \text{clip}(1 - \frac{\bar{\alpha}_t}{\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}, 0.999)$. The small offset $s$ prevents $\beta_t$ from being too small when $t$ is close to 0. This smoother schedule preserves structural information for a longer duration, improving negative log-likelihood metrics and overall generation quality.

\section{Diffusion Models Guidance}

To control the generation process—such as conditioning on class labels or text prompts—specific guidance techniques are employed.

\subsection{Classifier Guided Diffusion}

Dhariwal \& Nichol (2021) demonstrated that an explicitly trained classifier $f_\phi(y|\mathbf{x}_t)$ can guide the unconditional diffusion sampling process. By utilizing the gradients of the classifier $\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_t} \log f_\phi(y|\mathbf{x}_t)$, the sampling is steered toward the conditioning information $y$.

Using the score function of the joint distribution, the modified noise predictor becomes:
\begin{equation}
    \bar{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t) = \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t) - w \sqrt{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t} \nabla_{\mathbf{x}_t} \log f_\phi(y|\mathbf{x}_t)
\end{equation}
where $w$ is the gradient scale. The resulting Ablated Diffusion Model with classifier guidance (ADM-G) achieved better results than contemporary SOTA models like BigGAN.

\subsection{Classifier-Free Guidance (CFG)}

Training a robust time-dependent classifier is challenging. Ho \& Salimans (2021) introduced Classifier-Free Guidance, eliminating the need for a separate classifier. The core idea is to learn both a conditional score estimator $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t, y)$ and an unconditional estimator $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t, \emptyset)$ within a single neural network.

During training, the condition $y$ is randomly replaced with a learnable null token $\emptyset$ (e.g., $10\%$ of the time). During sampling, one prediction is made with the class token and another with the null token. These are extrapolated to amplify the conditioning signal:
\begin{align}
    \bar{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t, y) &= \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t, \emptyset) + w \left( \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t, y) - \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t, \emptyset) \right) \\
    &= (w+1)\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t, y) - w \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t, \emptyset)
\end{align}
CFG has become the standard mechanism for text-to-image models, drastically improving prompt adherence without the instability of external classifiers.

\section{Improving DDPM Sampling Speed}

A major drawback of DDPMs is the thousands of sequential steps required during inference.

\subsection{Progressive Distillation}
Salimans \& Ho (2022) proposed Progressive Distillation. It iteratively distills a trained deterministic teacher sampler into a new student model with halved sampling steps. The student model is trained to predict the outcome of taking two steps with the teacher model in a single step. Every distillation iteration halves the sampling steps, eventually allowing generation in extremely few steps.

\subsection{Consistency Models}
Song et al. (2023) introduced Consistency Models, learning to map intermediate noisy data points $\mathbf{x}_t$ directly back to their origin $\mathbf{x}_0$. Given a trajectory, a consistency function $f(\mathbf{x}_t, t) \mapsto \mathbf{x}_\epsilon$ guarantees that any point on the trajectory maps to the same origin: $f(\mathbf{x}_t, t) = f(\mathbf{x}_{t'}, t') = \mathbf{x}_\epsilon$. This allows for single-step generation, trading some compute during training for massive speedups at inference.

\section{Latent Diffusion Models (LDM)}

Running diffusion directly in high-resolution pixel space is computationally prohibitive. Rombach \& Blattmann (2022) introduced Latent Diffusion Models (LDM) which decompose the problem. Most bits of an image contribute to imperceptible high-frequency details. 

LDMs first trim off pixel-level redundancy using a highly optimized Autoencoder. An encoder $\mathcal{E}$ compresses the input image $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times W \times 3}$ into a smaller 2D latent vector $\mathbf{z} = \mathcal{E}(\mathbf{x}) \in \mathbb{R}^{h \times w \times c}$, with a downsampling rate $f = H/h = W/w = 2^m$. A decoder $\mathcal{D}$ reconstructs the image $\tilde{\mathbf{x}} = \mathcal{D}(\mathbf{z})$.

To avoid arbitrarily high variance in the latent space, LDMs employ either:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{KL-reg}: A small Kullback-Leibler penalty towards a standard normal distribution, similar to a VAE.
    \item \textbf{VQ-reg}: A vector quantization layer absorbed by the decoder.
\end{itemize}
The diffusion process (forward and reverse) is then exclusively executed in this highly compressed, semantically rich latent space $\mathbf{z}$, exponentially reducing computational cost.

\section{Architectures for Diffusion Models}

\subsection{U-Net Backbone}
Historically, the U-Net architecture has been the backbone of diffusion models. It features downsampling blocks, bottleneck blocks, and upsampling blocks connected via skip connections to prevent spatial information loss. 
Conditioning information such as the diffusion timestep $t$ is injected via sinusoidal embeddings into all blocks. External conditions like text are processed by a text embedder and injected using Cross-Attention mechanisms integrated within the ResNet and Self-Attention blocks.

\subsection{Transformer Backbone (DiT)}
Peebles \& Xie (2023) introduced the Diffusion Transformer (DiT), operating on latent patches. It replaces the U-Net with a standard Transformer architecture. The noise latent of size $I \times I \times C$ is ``patchified'' into patches of size $p$, converting it into a sequence of $(I/p)^2$ tokens. This sequence passes through Transformer blocks utilizing Adaptive Layer Normalization (adaLN) to condition on the timestep and class labels. The DiT decoder outputs the noise predictions and a diagonal covariance matrix prediction, showcasing that transformers scale exceptionally well for diffusion tasks without relying on convolutional inductive biases.

\section{Chapter Overview}

This chapter presented a comprehensive overview of diffusion models, a transformative approach in generative AI that formulates generation as a Markov chain of iterative denoising. Key topics included the mathematical foundations of the forward and reverse diffusion processes, and the transition from linear to cosine variance schedulers to better preserve structural information. To enable controllable generation, guidance mechanisms such as Classifier Guided Diffusion and Classifier-Free Guidance (CFG) were discussed. Furthermore, the chapter explored methods to address the slow inference speeds inherent to diffusion models, including Progressive Distillation and Consistency Models. Finally, modern architectural innovations like Latent Diffusion Models (LDM) and Diffusion Transformers (DiT) were detailed, highlighting how diffusion is effectively scaled to high-dimensional spaces like images.

\subsection{Possible Exam Questions}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Q: Explain the forward and reverse diffusion processes. Why is the exact reverse process intractable and how is it approximated?} \\
    \textbf{A:} The forward process is a Markov chain that gradually adds Gaussian noise to a data point over $T$ steps. The reverse process aims to denoise it to recreate a true sample. The exact reverse process is intractable because it requires evaluating the entire data distribution. Instead, it is approximated by learning a parameterized model $p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_{t-1} | \mathbf{x}_t)$, which is Gaussian for small step sizes.
    \item \textbf{Q: Derive the formulation for predicting the noise $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t)$ instead of the mean directly in the reverse process. Why is this beneficial?} \\
    \textbf{A:} Using Bayes' theorem on the forward posterior, the mean $\boldsymbol{\mu}_t$ is expressed as $\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}} \left( \mathbf{x}_t - \frac{1 - \alpha_t}{\sqrt{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t}} \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_t \right)$. By substituting $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_t$ with a neural network prediction $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t)$, the model predicts the noise. This simplifies the objective function and empirically yields superior sample quality by downweighting the loss at small $t$, focusing on difficult denoising tasks.
    \item \textbf{Q: What is the reparameterization trick in the context of diffusion models, and how does it allow sampling $\mathbf{x}_t$ at any arbitrary timestep $t$ in closed form?} \\
    \textbf{A:} The reparameterization trick allows expressing the noisy sample $\mathbf{x}_t$ recursively as a linear combination of $\mathbf{x}_0$ and cumulative Gaussian noise $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$. Specifically, $\mathbf{x}_t = \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t} \mathbf{x}_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t} \boldsymbol{\epsilon}$. This closed form permits sampling $\mathbf{x}_t$ efficiently at any step without explicitly iterating through all previous steps.
    \item \textbf{Q: Compare linear variance schedulers with cosine-based schedulers. Why does the cosine scheduler improve generation quality?} \\
    \textbf{A:} A linear scheduler destroys information too quickly, rendering the latter half of the forward process redundant as the image becomes pure noise early. A cosine scheduler offers a smoother variance progression that preserves structural information for a longer duration, preventing premature destruction and improving overall generation quality and log-likelihood metrics.
    \item \textbf{Q: Describe the difference between Classifier Guided Diffusion and Classifier-Free Guidance (CFG). What are the advantages of CFG over explicitly training a classifier?} \\
    \textbf{A:} Classifier Guided Diffusion steers generation using gradients from an explicitly trained, time-dependent classifier $f_\phi(y|\mathbf{x}_t)$. CFG eliminates the external classifier by jointly learning conditional and unconditional score estimators within a single network and amplifying their difference during sampling. CFG avoids the complexity and instability of training robust time-dependent classifiers while drastically improving prompt adherence.
    \item \textbf{Q: How do Progressive Distillation and Consistency Models address the slow inference speed of standard diffusion models?} \\
    \textbf{A:} Progressive Distillation iteratively trains a student model to predict the outcome of taking two steps with a teacher model in a single step, halving the sampling steps at each distillation phase. Consistency Models learn a mapping directly from any noisy state $\mathbf{x}_t$ to the origin $\mathbf{x}_0$, allowing for single-step or few-step generation.
    \item \textbf{Q: What is the main motivation behind Latent Diffusion Models (LDM)? Explain the roles of the encoder and decoder in this framework.} \\
    \textbf{A:} The motivation is to reduce the computational cost of running diffusion in high-resolution pixel space, recognizing that much of an image's detail is imperceptible high-frequency noise. The encoder compresses the image into a smaller, semantically rich 2D latent space where the diffusion process occurs. The decoder then reconstructs the full-resolution image from the latent representation.
    \item \textbf{Q: Contrast the traditional U-Net backbone with the Diffusion Transformer (DiT) architecture for diffusion models.} \\
    \textbf{A:} The traditional U-Net relies on convolutional inductive biases, downsampling/upsampling blocks, and skip connections, injecting conditionings via cross-attention. DiT replaces the U-Net with a standard Transformer by "patchifying" the latent space into a sequence of tokens. It uses Adaptive Layer Normalization (adaLN) for conditioning and shows that transformers scale exceptionally well for diffusion tasks without relying on convolutions.
\end{itemize}
