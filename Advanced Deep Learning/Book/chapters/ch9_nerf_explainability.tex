\chapter{Advanced Architectures: NeRF and Explainability}
\label{ch:advanced_architectures}

The integration of deep learning with classical computer graphics has catalyzed revolutionary paradigms in 3D scene representation and novel view synthesis. Concurrently, as neural networks grow exponentially in complexity, the necessity to rigorously interpret and explain their internal decision-making processes has become critical. This chapter provides a massive, comprehensive exploration of both Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) as an advanced generative architecture, and the mathematically rigorous methodologies underpinning neural network explainability.

\section{Deep Learning as 3D Scene Optimization}

Novel view synthesis requires the generation of a physically accurate, previously unseen perspective of a scene, given a finite set of input RGB images acquired by calibrated cameras. For these input images, we assume full knowledge of the intrinsic matrices $K_i$ and the extrinsic pose parameters $R_i, t_i$. Historically, this pipeline necessitated explicit geometric reconstruction: Structure from Motion (SfM) extracts sparse 3D point clouds and camera poses, Multi-View Stereo (MVS) densifies the point cloud (estimating a 3D coordinate for nearly every pixel), and surface reconstruction algorithms like Poisson surface reconstruction wrap these points into polygonal meshes. 

Computer graphics then executes the forward rendering problem: given explicit geometry, material properties, lighting conditions, and a virtual camera pose $K_{new}[R_{new} | t_{new}]$, it simulates light transport to yield a 2D image $I_{rendered}$.

\subsection{The Rendering Equation and Surface Reflectance}
The physical foundation of visual rendering is formalized by the rendering equation. The outgoing radiance $L_o(x, d_o)$ from a 3D surface point $x$ in a viewing direction $d_o$ is quantified by integrating the incoming radiance $L_i(x, d_i)$ across the hemisphere $\Omega$ of incident directions $d_i$:
\begin{equation}
L_o(x, d_o) = \int_\Omega f_r(x, d_i, d_o) L_i(x, d_i) \max(0, n(x)^\top d_i) d\omega
\end{equation}
where $n(x)$ is the surface normal vector at point $x$, and $f_r(x, d_i, d_o)$ is the Bidirectional Reflectance Distribution Function (BRDF), which encodes the material's properties by dictating how light is scattered.

For a perfectly diffuse, Lambertian surface, light scatters isotropically, rendering the BRDF a constant albedo $\rho$:
\begin{equation}
L(x, d_o) = \rho \max(0, n(x)^\top d_i)
\end{equation}
In contrast, realistic materials manifest complex specular highlights and view-dependent variations. The Blinn-Phong reflectance model approximates this by combining ambient, diffuse, and specular components:
\begin{equation}
L(x, d_o) = k_a c + k_d c \max(0, n(x)^\top d_i) + k_s \max(0, n(x)^\top h)^\alpha
\end{equation}
where $k_a, k_d, k_s$ are the ambient, diffuse, and specular reflection coefficients, respectively. The vector $h$ is the halfway vector, defined as $h = \frac{d_i + d_o}{||d_i + d_o||}$, and $\alpha$ controls the specular "shininess" (surface roughness).

\begin{insightbox}[The Transition to Neural Volumetric Rendering]
Explicit geometric models (e.g., meshes and voxel grids) are inherently discontinuous and notoriously difficult to optimize through gradient descent. Plenoptic approaches bypass explicit geometry by interpolating light rays across views, but suffer severely when handling complex occlusions. Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) elegantly combine these paradigms by parameterizing the plenoptic function implicitly within a continuous neural volume, effectively sidestepping the geometric discretization while supporting highly accurate, occlusion-aware differentiable rendering.
\end{insightbox}

\subsection{Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF)}

NeRF models a continuous scene using a fully connected deep network (a Multi-Layer Perceptron, MLP) that does not employ any convolutional layers. The network $F_\theta$ approximates a 5D vector function whose input is a 3D spatial location $x \in \mathbb{R}^3$ and a 2D viewing direction $d \in S^2$ (typically parameterized as a 3D Cartesian unit vector). The output is an emitted RGB color $c \in \mathbb{R}^3$ and a volume density $\sigma \in \mathbb{R}^+$:
\begin{equation}
F_\theta: \mathbb{R}^3 \times S^2 \to \mathbb{R}^3 \times \mathbb{R}^+, \quad (x, d) \mapsto (c, \sigma)
\end{equation}

By forcing the volume density $\sigma$ to be a function solely of the spatial location $x$ ($\sigma(x)$), NeRF ensures that the physical matter in the scene remains invariant to the observer's perspective. Conversely, by allowing the color $c(x, d)$ to vary with the viewing direction $d$, the architecture successfully synthesizes photorealistic, high-frequency view-dependent specularities and reflections.

\subsubsection{Differentiable Volume Rendering and Transmittance}

To synthesize an image from a given camera pose, NeRF casts virtual optical rays through the neural volume. A ray is parameterized as $r(t) = o + t d$, where $o$ is the camera origin, $d$ is the normalized viewing direction, and $t \in [t_n, t_f]$ bounds the ray.

We define a random variable $X$ representing the depth of the first light-blocking hit along the ray. Transmittance $T(t)$ is defined as the probability that the ray travels from $t_n$ to $t$ without being obstructed:
\begin{equation}
T(t) = \text{Prob}(X > t)
\end{equation}
Volume density $\sigma(t)$ represents the local rate of ray termination (opacity) at depth $t$:
\begin{equation}
\sigma(t) = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{\text{Prob}(t \le X < t + \Delta t | X > t)}{\Delta t}
\end{equation}
From these definitions, we can deduce the differential equation governing transmittance:
\begin{equation}
\text{Prob}(X \in [t, t + dt)) = T(t)\sigma(t)dt \implies T(t + dt) = T(t)(1 - \sigma(t)dt)
\end{equation}
This yields a first-order ODE $T'(t) = -\sigma(t)T(t)$, which integrates to:
\begin{equation}
T(t) = \exp\left( - \int_{t_n}^t \sigma(s) ds \right)
\end{equation}
The expected color $C(r)$ rendered for the ray $r$ is the integral of the emitted color weighted by the probability of the ray terminating at exactly depth $t$:
\begin{equation}
C(r) = \int_{t_n}^{t_f} T(t) \sigma(r(t)) c(r(t), d) dt
\end{equation}

To render this computationally tractable, the continuous integral is discretized using stratified sampling. The range $[t_n, t_f]$ is partitioned into $N$ bins of length $\delta_i = t_{i+1} - t_i$. Assuming density and color remain constant within each discrete segment, the integral reduces to a differentiable summation:
\begin{equation}
\hat{C}(r) = \sum_{i=1}^N T_i \alpha_i c_i
\end{equation}
where $\alpha_i$ represents the opacity of segment $i$:
\begin{equation}
\alpha_i = 1 - \exp(-\sigma_i \delta_i)
\end{equation}
and the discrete transmittance $T_i$ is the accumulated probability of not hitting any particle before segment $i$:
\begin{equation}
T_i = \prod_{j=1}^{i-1} (1 - \alpha_j)
\end{equation}

The network parameters $\theta$ are trained purely via a photometric loss against ground-truth pixels $C_{gt}(r)$:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \sum_{r \in \mathcal{R}} ||\hat{C}_\theta(r) - C_{gt}(r)||^2_2
\end{equation}
This loss is minimized using gradient descent optimizers such as Adam.

\subsubsection{Positional Encoding and Hierarchical Sampling}
Deep neural networks exhibit a profound spectral bias towards low-frequency functions, meaning a naive $F_\theta$ struggles to represent the sharp gradients of real-world textures and geometric boundaries. NeRF resolves this by applying a high-frequency positional encoding function $\gamma(\cdot)$ to both $x$ and $d$ before feeding them into the network:
\begin{equation}
\gamma(p) = \left(\sin(2^0 \pi p), \cos(2^0 \pi p), \dots, \sin(2^{L-1} \pi p), \cos(2^{L-1} \pi p)\right)
\end{equation}
In standard implementations, $L=10$ is used for spatial coordinates $x$, and $L=4$ is used for directional inputs $d$.

To drastically improve rendering efficiency, NeRF employs hierarchical volume sampling. Evaluating the MLP uniformly across empty space wastes massive computational resources. Instead, a \textit{coarse} network evaluates a set of $N_c$ uniformly sampled points. The resulting weights $w_i = T_i \alpha_i$ are normalized to formulate a piecewise-constant probability density function (PDF):
\begin{equation}
\hat{w}_i = \frac{w_i}{\sum_{j=1}^{N_c} w_j}
\end{equation}
Using inverse transform sampling on this PDF, a second set of $N_f$ points is sampled precisely where visual matter is concentrated. A larger \textit{fine} network is then evaluated at all $N_c + N_f$ points to compute the final photorealistic render. The total loss is the sum of both the coarse and fine photometric errors.

\subsection{Gaussian Splatting: Explicit Volumetric Representation}
While NeRF provides supreme differentiable rendering, its computational cost during inference is immense, demanding millions of MLP evaluations per frame. 3D Gaussian Splatting reimagines volumetric synthesis by abandoning the implicit continuous MLP in favor of an explicit set of 3D Gaussian primitives initialized via SfM (e.g., COLMAP). 

Each Gaussian primitive encompasses a 3D position $\mu$, a 3D covariance matrix $\Sigma$, an opacity factor $\alpha$, and spherical harmonics (SH) coefficients representing view-dependent color. Rather than ray-marching, Gaussian Splatting achieves real-time rendering through an ultra-fast tile-based rasterizer. It projects the 3D covariances into 2D, sorts the primitives by depth, and employs an alpha-blending routine (sort $\to$ splat $\to$ blend) that preserves strict differentiability, achieving state-of-the-art visual quality with drastically superior inference efficiency.

\section{Explainability in Deep Neural Networks}

Modern Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) operate as hyper-dimensional black boxes with millions of opaque parameters. Deploying such systems in mission-critical environments (e.g., healthcare, autonomous driving) demands extreme scrutiny to identify "Clever Hans" phenomena---situations where a model achieves high accuracy by exploiting dataset biases rather than genuine semantic features (e.g., classifying an image as "Husky" merely because of snow in the background, rather than canine morphology).

\subsection{Class Activation Mapping (CAM)}
Saliency maps offer a foundational mechanism for explainability by projecting visual heatmaps onto the original image. Class Activation Mapping (CAM) generates these explanations by leveraging the Global Average Pooling (GAP) layers intrinsic to specific architectures. Let $f_k(x, y)$ be the spatial activation of the $k$-th feature map in the final convolutional layer, and let $w_k^c$ be the weight connecting the $k$-th feature map to the final output node for class $c$. The CAM $M_c(x, y)$ is:
\begin{equation}
M_c(x, y) = \sum_k w_k^c f_k(x, y)
\end{equation}
While elegant, CAM mandates a strict structural configuration---replacing fully connected layers with GAP layers---which invariably causes catastrophic drops in classification accuracy (often losing 90\% of fully connected parameter capacity) and lacks portability across diverse architectures.

\subsection{Gradient-Based Attribution Methods}

Gradient attribution establishes a formal mapping $S_c: \mathbb{R}^{H \times W \times C} \to [0,1]$ and defines a spatial attribution map $A(x) = \{a_i\}_{i=1}^n$ representing the localized contribution of each pixel $x_i$ to the predicted scalar output. Leveraging a first-order Taylor expansion around the input, we approximate the network:
\begin{equation}
S_c(x) \approx S_c(x_0) + \nabla S_c(x_0)^\top (x - x_0) \implies w = \frac{\partial S_c}{\partial x}(I)
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Vanilla Gradients and Integrated Gradients}
Vanilla Gradients evaluate $\frac{\partial S_c}{\partial x_i}(I)$ directly via backpropagation. Unfortunately, because of deep network saturation (where local derivatives approach zero) and shattering gradient problems, these maps often manifest as incomprehensible, high-frequency noise.

Integrated Gradients solves the saturation dilemma by computing the path integral of the gradients. We define an information-less baseline image $I'$ (e.g., a pure black matrix). The attribution is aggregated along a linear interpolation sequence between $I'$ and the target input $I$:
\begin{equation}
\text{IntegratedGrad}_{c,i}(I) = (I_i - I'_i) \int_0^1 \frac{\partial S_c}{\partial x_i}(I' + \alpha(I - I')) d\alpha
\end{equation}
This mathematically satisfies the Completeness Property, proving that the sum of local attributions strictly equals the global output difference:
\begin{equation}
\sum_{i} \text{IntegratedGrad}_{c,i}(I) = S_c(I) - S_c(I')
\end{equation}
In practical implementations, the continuous integral is replaced by a discrete Riemann sum over $m$ steps (typically $m \approx 50$):
\begin{equation}
\text{IntegratedGrad}_{c,i}(I) \approx (I_i - I'_i) \frac{1}{m} \sum_{j=1}^m \frac{\partial S_c}{\partial x_i}\left(I' + \frac{j}{m}(I - I')\right)
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{SmoothGrad}
SmoothGrad functions as an inference-time photometric augmentation algorithm designed to denoise local gradient approximations. It perturbs the original image $I$ with additive Gaussian noise $\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$ across $n$ independent trials, subsequently averaging the resulting gradient matrices:
\begin{equation}
\text{NoisyGrad}_{c,i}(I) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{j=1}^n \frac{\partial S_c}{\partial x_i}(I + \epsilon_j)
\end{equation}
Hyperparameter tuning typically sets the sample size $n=50$, with the noise standard deviation $\sigma$ scaled to $10\% - 20\%$ of the dynamic intensity range $(\max(I) - \min(I))$. SmoothGrad seamlessly interfaces with Integrated Gradients and Guided Backpropagation, dramatically enhancing the signal-to-noise ratio of visual attributions.

\subsection{Gradient-Weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM)}

Grad-CAM democratizes the CAM methodology by eliminating the strict architectural prerequisites. Instead of extracting explicit neural weights, it computes empirical importance weights $w_k^c$ by executing a Global Average Pooling directly over the gradient tensors flowing back to the final convolutional feature maps:
\begin{equation}
w_k^c = \frac{1}{nm} \sum_i \sum_j \frac{\partial S_c}{\partial a_k(i, j)}
\end{equation}
where $a_k(i, j)$ represents the spatial activation matrix. Once the weights are determined, Grad-CAM fuses the feature maps and applies a strict ReLU activation:
\begin{equation}
g^c = \text{ReLU}\left(\sum_k w_k^c a_k\right)
\end{equation}
The ReLU operator acts as a crucial filtering mechanism, isolating only those distinct spatial activations that positively impact the desired class $c$, nullifying negative suppressive features. The extreme flexibility of Grad-CAM means $S_c$ does not strictly have to be a classification logit; it can represent any differentiable target, including textual coordinates in Image Captioning architectures or distinct decision pathways in Visual Question Answering (VQA) networks.

Furthermore, \textbf{Grad-CAM++} refines this by utilizing second- and third-order derivatives to formulate the importance weights $w_k^c$. This higher-order analytical perspective severely enhances the spatial resolution and localization accuracy in image domains saturated with multiple interacting instances of the same object class.

\subsubsection{Augmented Grad-CAM and Heatmap Super-Resolution}
Because Grad-CAM extracts features from the deepest convolutional layers, its spatial resolution is inherently constrained (e.g., to a dense $14 \times 14$ grid). Augmented Grad-CAM synthesizes a high-resolution attribution map by executing multiple forward passes using geometrically perturbed (rotated, translated) input vectors.

Let the augmented transformations be denoted by operators $\mathcal{A}_l: \mathbb{R}^{N \times M} \to \mathbb{R}^{N \times M}$. The process of low-resolution feature extraction is modeled mathematically as a downsampling operator $\mathcal{D}$. Augmented Grad-CAM recovers the optimal high-resolution heatmap $h$ by defining an inverse super-resolution problem:
\begin{equation}
\arg\min_h \frac{1}{2} \sum_{l=1}^L ||\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A}_l(h)) - g_l||_2^2 + \lambda TV_{\ell_1}(h) + \frac{\mu}{2} ||h||_2^2
\end{equation}
To strictly preserve edge discontinuities in the attribution map, the objective enforces an Anisotropic Total Variation regularization penalty:
\begin{equation}
TV_{\ell_1}(h) = \sum_{i,j} \left( ||\partial_x h(i,j)|| + ||\partial_y h(i,j)|| \right)
\end{equation}
Due to the non-smooth absolute value formulations in the $L_1$ space, the objective is minimized iteratively leveraging Subgradient Descent techniques.

\subsection{Perturbation-Based Attributions and Perception Visualization}
To completely sidestep the shattering gradients phenomena, perturbation algorithms measure attribution exclusively via forward-pass masking:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{RISE (Randomized Input Sampling for Explanation):} Synthesizes $N$ random, smooth binary masks $M_i$. Each mask occludes the input image $I$, producing an output probability $p_i$ corresponding to the target class $c$. The saliency map is reconstructed as the probabilistically weighted expectation of these masks:
    \begin{equation}
    S = \frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^N M_i} \sum_{i=1}^N p_i \cdot M_i
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Score-CAM:} Escapes random masking by relying directly on the network's intrinsic feature maps $h_k^c$. These maps are upsampled, normalized into the $[0,1]$ domain, and projected onto the original input image. The importance weight $w_k^c$ is computed as the raw inferential difference:
    \begin{equation}
    w_k^c = \text{CNN}(h_k^c \circ X) - \text{CNN}(X_b)
    \end{equation}
    where $X_b$ constitutes the baseline image matrix.
\end{itemize}

Finally, \textbf{Perception Visualization (PV)} constructs a deterministic bridge between semantic explanation (what) and spatial localization (where). Given an encoder network $E$, PV introduces a specialized decoder $D$ optimized explicitly to invert and reconstruct the latent feature representations into visual space. By calculating Grad-CAM coordinates on these reconstructed artifacts, PV delivers unparalleled visual insight, systematically unraveling why a CNN catastrophically misclassified a train traversing a bridge as a "boat", exposing the precise sub-textures that deceived the convolutional layers.

\section{Chapter Overview}

This chapter provided a comprehensive exploration of two crucial themes in modern deep learning: neural 3D scene representation and neural network explainability. We first discussed the foundations of novel view synthesis, moving from explicit geometric reconstruction to the implicit, continuous volumetric rendering approach of Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF). We also examined 3D Gaussian Splatting as an explicit and highly efficient alternative to NeRF's computationally expensive ray-marching.

In the second half, the chapter focused on unraveling the "black box" nature of Convolutional Neural Networks. We explored saliency maps starting from Class Activation Mapping (CAM), progressing into gradient-based attribution methods such as Integrated Gradients and SmoothGrad, which mathematically address issues like gradient saturation and noise. We further detailed Grad-CAM and its augmented variants, concluding with perturbation-based approaches like RISE and Score-CAM that avoid reliance on gradients altogether.
